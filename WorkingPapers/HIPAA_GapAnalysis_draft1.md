# Phase 2a HIPAA Gap Analysis

## How to Read This Memo

A persistent source of confusion about HIPAA is the difference between what the law *requires*, what it *permits*, and what individual providers *choose* to do as a matter of policy. These three categories do not line up. HIPAA sets a floor of minimum privacy protection, not a ceiling. Providers may — and routinely do — restrict disclosures more narrowly than HIPAA requires. This memo distinguishes those categories throughout. Where it matters, the memo also distinguishes between authorization (giving someone the right to receive information) and legal authority to make decisions (a separate and more powerful status with different requirements).

---

## Section 1 — How HIPAA Creates Practical Barriers for Aging Support Networks

### The basic structure

The HIPAA Privacy Rule (45 CFR Part 164) prohibits covered entities — providers, health plans, and their business associates — from disclosing protected health information (PHI) without either the individual's authorization or an applicable regulatory exception. PHI is broadly defined: it includes not just diagnosis and treatment records but any health information that identifies or could identify an individual.

The default is non-disclosure. Unless a disclosure fits within an enumerated exception or the patient has authorized it, the provider may not release the information. This structure was designed to protect patient privacy, and it does that well. The problem is that it also cuts off the informal support networks that aging individuals rely on precisely when those networks are most needed.

### Who is inside and outside the default circle

Treatment-related disclosures among providers directly involved in care are permitted without patient authorization. A primary care physician can share information with a specialist. A hospital can share discharge information with a home health agency. The coordination chain among clinical providers works.

Family members, friends, and neighbors are outside that chain. Unless the patient has taken affirmative steps to authorize disclosure, providers cannot share PHI with them — including basic status information. The neighbor who drove the patient to the emergency room cannot be told whether the admission was serious. The adult child calling from Seattle cannot be told what the working diagnosis is or when discharge is expected. The close friend coordinating meals and transportation during a recovery cannot confirm whether a particular medication affects driving. All of this is blocked by default.

### The provider-default problem

The barrier is compounded by provider behavior. HIPAA's family-involvement provisions (discussed in Section 2) create genuine flexibility for disclosures to informal support networks. Most providers do not use that flexibility. They default to treating any disclosure outside the direct treatment chain as prohibited unless the patient has filed a formal written authorization. This is often not what HIPAA requires, but it is what providers do — partly from caution, partly from incomplete understanding of the regulations, and partly from institutional risk management. The practical result for patients and their support networks is that the regulatory permission that exists is largely invisible at the point of care.

### The fragmentation problem

Even when a patient has executed authorizations, the authorizations do not travel. Each covered entity — each hospital, each physician practice, each pharmacy, each specialist group — is legally distinct. An authorization filed with a primary care physician does not bind the hospital where that physician admits patients. An authorization filed with one hospital does not bind another. A patient with five providers needs five separate authorizations on file to give a support person consistent access.

On a small island with limited local providers, this is somewhat less acute — the number of distinct covered entities a Lopez Island resident interacts with locally may be small. But the ferry-distance medical system matters here: mainland hospitals, specialists in Anacortes, Bellingham, or Seattle, and any care received away from Lopez Island are all separate covered entities with no connection to whatever the patient filed locally.

### The incapacity transition — where the gap is most acute

The Homeostasis Framework identifies the transition from individual agency to reliance on informal and formal support as the central challenge of the middle arc of aging decline. This transition is precisely where HIPAA's barriers are most dangerous.

When a person has capacity, she can authorize disclosures in real time. She is present. She can say "yes, talk to my daughter." She can fill out forms. The system, imperfectly, accommodates this.

When she loses capacity — whether suddenly (a stroke, a fall with serious injury) or gradually — the ability to authorize disclosures in real time is gone. Whatever she put in place before that transition controls. If she put nothing in place, the default prevails: the support network is largely cut off at the moment it is most needed. Family members may be present in the hospital room and still unable to receive systematic information from clinical staff because they have not been formally designated.

The incapacity window is the specific problem the workshop needs to prepare participants to close before it opens.

### What "basic health status information" means in practice

It is worth being precise about what the support network actually needs. In most situations, informal caregivers do not need detailed diagnostic or treatment records. They need operational information: Is she in the hospital? When might she be discharged? Are there medication changes affecting how she should be cared for at home? Can she safely be left alone overnight? Is the prognosis serious enough that family should travel?

This is the minimum coordination information that informal support networks need to function. HIPAA's structure, combined with provider defaults, makes even this basic information difficult to obtain without prior authorization on file.

## Section 2 — What Authorizations Exist Under Federal HIPAA

Three distinct mechanisms allow information sharing beyond the clinical treatment chain. They differ significantly in what they authorize, who they reach, and what they do not cover.

### 2a. 45 CFR 164.510(b) — Informal permission for family and friend involvement

This provision permits (does not require) covered entities to share PHI with a family member, other relative, close personal friend, or any person identified by the individual as involved in the individual's care or payment for care.

**When it applies.** The individual must either be present and agree (expressly or by failing to object), or the provider must exercise professional judgment that the individual would want the disclosure to occur. If the individual is incapacitated, the provider may share information if it determines — in its professional judgment — that doing so is in the individual's best interest.

**What it covers.** Information directly relevant to the person's involvement in care or payment. A neighbor coordinating transportation and meals can receive information relevant to those functions. An adult child coordinating discharge planning can receive discharge-relevant information. The scope is defined by the role, not by a blanket designation.

**What it does not cover.** This provision requires some basis for the provider to make the judgment — either the individual's agreement or the provider's professional judgment. It does not create an enforceable right for family members or friends to demand information. The provider retains discretion. In practice, if the provider's institutional policy defaults to non-disclosure, the flexibility 164.510(b) creates is rarely exercised. This provision also does not give anyone the authority to make health care decisions — it is only an authorization for information sharing.

**Key point for the workshop.** 164.510(b) is the low-friction pathway that participants can activate right now, with minimal paperwork, simply by telling their providers — in their next appointment — who is involved in their care and what information those people can receive. Whether the provider documents and honors this varies. This pathway is real but unreliable.

### 2b. HIPAA Authorization — 45 CFR 164.508

A written HIPAA Authorization permits a covered entity to use or disclose PHI for purposes specified in the authorization. It is the most explicit and durable form of consent for information sharing.

**Required elements.** A valid authorization must identify: (1) the PHI to be used or disclosed (specific description); (2) the person(s) authorized to make the disclosure; (3) the person(s) to whom disclosure may be made; (4) a description of the purpose; (5) an expiration date or event; and (6) the individual's signature and date. It must also include specific statements about the individual's right to revoke and the consequences (if any) of refusing to sign.

**What it covers.** An authorization, once on file with a covered entity, gives the named recipients the right to receive the specified PHI from that covered entity. It is a direct, documented consent that cuts through provider default practices. The named recipient can call or present the authorization and receive the information.

**What it does not cover.** Four significant limitations.

First, authorization is provider-specific. A HIPAA Authorization filed with one covered entity does not bind any other covered entity. Each provider needs its own authorization on file.

Second, authorization authorizes disclosure — it does not authorize decision-making. The person named in the authorization can receive information; they cannot make health care decisions on the patient's behalf unless they have a separate legal designation to do so.

Third, there is no national repository or interoperable system. There is no mechanism for a patient to file one authorization that covers all her providers. And there is no way for a support person to check whether they are authorized at a given provider without contacting that provider directly.

Fourth, while the authorization is in force, it cannot be automatically revoked by incapacity — but the practical ability to update or expand it disappears when the individual loses capacity. Whatever was on file before the transition is what applies.

### 2c. Personal Representative Designation — 45 CFR 164.502(g)

A personal representative under HIPAA has the same rights as the individual to access PHI and to authorize its disclosure. This is the most powerful of the three mechanisms — it creates full informational parity with the patient.

**How it is established.** For adults, HIPAA defines personal representative by reference to state law: whoever has legal authority to make health care decisions for the individual under state law is that individual's personal representative for HIPAA purposes. In Washington, this is the individual's health care agent appointed under an Advance Directive (see Section 4), or a court-appointed guardian with authority over health care decisions. A financial power of attorney does not create HIPAA personal representative status; only health care decision-making authority does.

**What it covers.** Everything the patient would have access to. The personal representative can access records, receive information from all clinical staff, sign authorizations, and request amendments. From HIPAA's perspective, the personal representative is standing in the patient's shoes.

**What it does not cover.** Three significant limitations.

First, legal authority may not be established until incapacity. An advance directive health care agent typically has no authority while the individual retains capacity — the agent's authority is triggered by the individual's incapacity. A personal representative who cannot yet exercise authority because the patient is not yet incapacitated is not, in practice, acting as a personal representative during that period.

Second, providers can require documentation. A provider can ask for evidence of the legal designation before treating someone as a personal representative. In a crisis situation, if the documentation is not immediately available, access may be delayed.

Third, the personal representative designation still produces separate coverage at each covered entity. There is no central registration. The agent showing up at a new provider needs to establish her authority at that provider.

## Section 3 — What Participants Can Actually Do

### Tier 1 actions: Low friction, limited durability

**Verbal designation under 164.510(b).** At the next appointment with any provider, the patient can tell the provider who is involved in her care and what information they can receive. Some providers document this in the chart; others do not. This is immediate and costs nothing but is only as durable as the provider's documentation practices.

**Ask about the provider's HIPAA authorization form.** Every major health system, hospital, and most large practices have their own HIPAA authorization form. Participants can ask for and complete the form at their next visit. This takes approximately 10–15 minutes and produces a durable, documented record at that specific provider.

### Tier 2 actions: More durable, requiring some preparation

**Inventory providers and file authorizations with each.** The durable solution is to map every relevant provider — primary care, pharmacy, specialists, hospital — and file a completed HIPAA authorization at each. For most participants, the relevant local provider set is small. The challenge is identifying all the providers and remembering to add new ones as the provider list changes.

**Identify who the authorizations should cover.** Participants need to think through not just family but the actual support network: the neighbor who may be the first responder in a crisis; the close friend who coordinates logistics; the adult child who manages care from a distance. These people should all be named.

### Tier 3 actions: Most comprehensive, requiring legal documents

**Execute an Advance Directive naming a health care agent.** This is the only mechanism that creates a personal representative under HIPAA with access to everything, across all providers, without requiring a separate authorization at each. The agent has HIPAA personal representative status automatically once the designation is made and the relevant authority has been activated. In Washington, Advance Directives are governed by RCW 70.122. Completing one does not require an attorney, though complexity of the person's situation may make legal review advisable.

**Ensure the agent has the documents.** Designation is necessary but not sufficient. The agent must have a copy of the Advance Directive and must know she is designated. This sounds obvious but is routinely not done.

### Persistent limitations across all tiers

The following limitations are not fully resolvable by participant action:

- **Provider policy variation.** Some providers restrict disclosure beyond what HIPAA requires regardless of what is on file. There is no mechanism to compel compliance short of complaint or litigation, neither of which is practical in a crisis.
- **Authorization fragmentation.** No single action covers all providers. Participants must identify all their providers and maintain authorizations as the provider list changes.
- **The incapacity timing problem.** During the "slow decline" period the Homeostasis Framework describes, the patient may still have formal capacity even while practically unable to manage authorizations. The health care agent's authority has not yet been triggered. The patient may also be unable to make new authorizations reliably. This window — not yet legally incapacitated, but increasingly unable to manage self-care — is the most difficult to plan for.
- **Mental health and substance use disorder records.** These categories have additional protections beyond standard medical records (see Section 4). Standard HIPAA authorizations may not be sufficient to release them.
- **No automatic alert mechanism.** Even when authorizations are in place, they are passive — they authorize disclosure if asked but do not trigger any outreach to the support network. The support network must know to call, and must know which provider to call.

## Section 4 — Washington State Law

### General preemption principle

HIPAA contains a preemption provision: federal HIPAA preempts state law that is less protective of patient privacy. State law controls when it is more protective than HIPAA. Washington law in several areas is more protective, and those areas are material to this workshop.

### Advance Directives and health care agents — RCW 70.122

Washington's Health Care Directives statute (RCW 70.122) governs the Advance Directive (sometimes called a Directive to Physicians or a Living Will) and the designation of a health care agent. A health care agent designated in a valid Washington Advance Directive is a personal representative for HIPAA purposes.

Key practical points:

- Washington Advance Directives do not require an attorney to prepare, but they require the signature of the declarant and two adult witnesses who are not the health care agent, not related to the declarant by blood or marriage, not entitled to any portion of the declarant's estate, and not a health care provider directly involved in the declarant's care.
- Washington does not require notarization for an Advance Directive, though some institutions may prefer it.
- The health care agent's authority is generally triggered when a physician determines the patient lacks capacity to make health care decisions. This means the agent does not function as a personal representative under HIPAA during periods when the patient has capacity.

**The capacity-trigger problem, specific to Washington.** Washington's statute requires physician determination of incapacity before the agent's authority activates (RCW 70.122.030). This creates a gap for the "slow decline" scenario: a patient who is functionally impaired but not formally adjudicated as lacking capacity may have an agent who has no authority yet. HIPAA authorizations (Section 2b) are the mechanism that fills this gap during this period. This is a key coordination point for workshop participants: the Advance Directive covers the incapacity endpoint; HIPAA authorizations must be in place before that point.

### Mental health records — RCW 71.05.390

Washington law governing involuntary treatment records and mental health information is more protective than HIPAA. Mental health records of patients who have been subjected to involuntary treatment have strict confidentiality protections under RCW 71.05.390. Even with a valid HIPAA authorization or a personal representative designation, these records may not be releasable without additional process. Providers treating mental health conditions voluntarily apply similar caution under general Washington health records law (RCW 70.02).

**Practical implication for the workshop.** Participants should not assume that a standard HIPAA authorization form covers mental health records. If mental health care is part of a participant's health picture, a specific inquiry to those providers about their disclosure policies is warranted. Do not overstate this to participants — most will not have mental health records in the involuntary treatment system — but flag it as a category where standard forms may not be sufficient.

### Substance use disorder records — 42 CFR Part 2 (federal, not state)

This is a federal category, not Washington-specific, but it is frequently misunderstood. Records from providers treating substance use disorders under federal funding are governed by 42 CFR Part 2, which is stricter than HIPAA. A standard HIPAA authorization does not authorize disclosure of these records; a 42 CFR Part 2-compliant authorization with specific program consent is required. The 2024 revisions to Part 2 aligned it more closely with HIPAA for treatment, payment, and health care operations purposes, but the separate consent requirement for disclosure to non-treating third parties (including family support networks) remains. This is an area of ongoing regulatory evolution.

### Vulnerable Adult protections and mandatory reporting — RCW 74.34

Washington's Vulnerable Adult statute authorizes Adult Protective Services (APS) to investigate abuse, neglect, abandonment, or financial exploitation of vulnerable adults. HIPAA's exception at 45 CFR 164.512(c) permits providers to disclose to APS without patient authorization. This provision works in the direction of disclosure when there is suspicion of harm. It is not a mechanism participants can use to expand their support network's access under normal circumstances, but it is relevant background for the workshop's discussion of when formal systems become involved.

### Washington POLST — RCW 43.70.480

Washington's POLST (Portable Medical Order) is a physician order that travels with the patient across care settings. It does not directly modify HIPAA's information-sharing rules. It is relevant to the workshop in the sudden-incapacity scenario but is not a mechanism for expanding the support network's information access.

### Washington surrogate hierarchy — RCW 70.122.040

Washington recognizes a surrogate hierarchy for situations where an individual lacks capacity and has not named a health care agent: (1) court-appointed guardian; (2) spouse or registered domestic partner; (3) adult children; (4) parents; (5) adult siblings; (6) other adult relatives; (7) close friends. This surrogate would become a HIPAA personal representative under the applicable tier.

**Key limitation.** The surrogate hierarchy only activates when the individual lacks capacity and has not designated an agent. It does not give family members access to information during the patient's capacity period, and it does not apply cleanly if there is any dispute about the surrogate designation. For participants with complex family situations — estrangements, multiple children with differing views — reliance on the default surrogate hierarchy is a significant risk. Explicit designation via Advance Directive is strongly preferable.

## Areas of Uncertainty and Flagged Complexity

**Provider compliance variance.** The single most significant uncertainty in this analysis is that provider behavior is highly variable and largely unpredictable in advance. Even where regulatory permission exists, institutions implement it differently. The analysis above describes what the law allows; it cannot reliably describe what a given provider will actually do in a given situation. This limitation should be explicitly acknowledged in workshop content.

**The capacity-trigger gap in Washington's Advance Directive statute.** Washington's requirement that a physician formally determine incapacity before the health care agent's authority activates creates a gap during the slow-decline period. This is the most important regulatory complexity for the workshop to address, and it is not fully resolvable by participant action. HIPAA authorizations bridge the gap partially, but only to the extent they have been filed with each relevant provider and only to the extent the named recipients know which provider to contact.

**42 CFR Part 2 revisions.** The 2024 revisions to federal substance use disorder record rules are recent enough that many providers have not fully implemented updated policies. Analysis of what participants can actually obtain in this category should be treated as uncertain until provider-specific policies are clarified. The workshop should flag the category without making specific claims about what will or will not be accessible.

**Washington law scope.** This memo addresses Washington State provisions most directly material to the workshop. A full survey of Washington health privacy law is beyond its scope. If content development surfaces specific scenarios — a participant with significant mental health history, a participant involved in the state's Medicaid long-term care system — those scenarios should be returned to the HIPAA Advisor for targeted analysis.

## Key Findings Summary

The central regulatory finding is that HIPAA's barriers for aging support networks are real but substantially self-imposed by provider behavior: the law permits considerably more disclosure to family and informal caregivers than providers routinely allow. The most actionable participant interventions are (1) filing HIPAA authorizations with each relevant provider now, naming the actual support network rather than only formal family members, and (2) executing a Washington Advance Directive naming a health care agent, which becomes a HIPAA personal representative at incapacity. Washington law adds one significant complication — the Advance Directive agent's authority does not activate until a physician determines incapacity, creating a gap during the slow-decline period that HIPAA authorizations must fill. Mental health records and substance use disorder records are categories where standard authorizations may be insufficient.
